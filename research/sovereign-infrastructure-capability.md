Output of a multi-agent research run, 28 August 2026. Six research angles (159 findings),
a synthesis pass, two adversarial reviews, and a final consolidation.

## Recommendation: `fold_into_existing_pillar`

**Mission-Critical & Computing Facilities (a named service line inside Pillar 01 Defence Engineering Design — not a fifth pillar)**

_Power, cooling, earthing and physical-security engineering for computing facilities, measured against the facility clauses of Nigeria's 2026 cloud instruments — sold as surveys, studies and remediation design, not as data centre construction._

### Reasoning

Both adversarial reviewers independently concluded "do not publish a fifth pillar", from different lenses, and their reasoning holds up. I am not defaulting to "add it".

The technical demand thesis is real, and I verified it myself rather than taking the proposal's word — which materially changed my confidence. All three NITDA instruments are on nitda.gov.ng/regulations/. I extracted the text of all three and confirmed verbatim: §13.1(I) Tier III / Rated-3 for design and constructed facility for Level 3 and Level 4; §13.2 N+1 on UPS, PDUs and generators plus annual PUE reporting; §13.3 three distinct authentication factors, mandatory 24x7x365 on-site security, 90-day CCTV retention; §10.4 primary and secondary sites in Nigeria and in different geopolitical zones for Level 2 and above, with Level 4 never leaving Nigeria; Level 4 defined to include military intelligence and required to sit on-premises or in a NITDA-certified private cloud, to standards defined and audited by ONSA; NDIAF §11.3 Design Assurance Review before construction with §11.3(c) expressly not replacing construction approval under other laws; NDIAF Schedule 1 S1.3 capital and insurance thresholds exactly as stated; Annex H Category 1 GREEN including "demonstrable use of local construction, engineering, and facilities management firms"; both instruments signed 4 August 2026, effective 1 January 2027. The regulatory case is stronger than a normal pitch's evidence base.

But the demand thesis was never the problem. Three things decide this.

First, the firm cannot transact. A Federal Public Institution generally cannot contract or pay a firm absent from the BPP database with current statutory compliance. BPP registration, COREN firm registration, professional indemnity cover and the limitation-of-liability clause are all unresolved placeholders in PPEC's own generator today. So nothing in the original "credible now" list is procurable now, and publishing a fifth pillar advertises availability the firm does not have. The proposal treated these as credential bullets; they are the gate on the entire timeline.

Second, breadth is discounted, not rewarded. Five pillars and roughly twenty-four service lines, from a firm with zero completed defence contracts and a leadership page that is a structural placeholder with six unnamed roles, reads as a brochure chasing whatever is funded. Ten sub-capabilities spanning electrical, mechanical, fire, electronic security, structured cabling, commissioning management, IoT, geotechnical siting, supply chain and O&M is a fifteen-to-twenty-five-person practice. I cut it to four, all of them existing MEP and asset-management competence.

Third, asymmetry of the strategy itself. "Precision substitutes for portfolio" only works if it is airtight, and reviewing the proposal against the primary sources I found two real errors in the very citations meant to be the differentiator: the positioning paragraph equated Tier III with N+1 when Tier III additionally requires concurrent maintainability of distribution paths; and the claim that the Technical Guideline names government contractors and designers among its addressees is not supported — its Application and Scope names FPIs, CSPs, SIs, data centre operators and infrastructure providers, and nothing else. I also found the Operational Manual precedence claim inverted: NDIAF Schedule 4 S4.3 says the Framework prevails over the Manual, not the reverse. Two of those would have been caught by any Uptime-literate reader in a first meeting, which is exactly the failure mode of a strategy that trades portfolio for precision.

Folding it into Pillar 01 keeps nearly all the positioning value — the same clauses, the same standards, the same insight article, which is the strongest single credibility instrument available to a firm with no public references — while avoiding an edit surface of eight-plus files including the hand-authored homepage rotator, avoiding the breadth discount, and preserving reversibility. A service line can be promoted to a pillar the day a reference exists. A pillar cannot be quietly demoted.

One point where I depart from the reviewers: the commercial reviewer wanted two sub-capabilities, the buyer four. I kept four, because compliance gap assessment and the asset register are genuinely existing Pillar 04 competence and are the natural door-openers, but I have marked earthing/surge and standby power as the lead offers because they are the only two that are referenceable in weeks and saleable outside the federal procurement gate.

I also left one item on the sell-now list that the buyer reviewer wanted removed entirely: discipline-specific review of the electrical, earthing and mechanical packages. I narrowed it as they suggested rather than deleting it, because it is a real and defensible sub-mandate. Owner's engineer as a full mandate, independent commissioning, colocation fit-out in a live hall, licensed installation, and Tier III switching-procedure training are all removed — the last one deleted outright rather than deferred, since teaching switching procedures on critical plant from a firm that has never operated a data hall invites a training-induced outage the firm would rightly be blamed for.

### Positioning paragraph (draft)

> Nigeria's National Cloud Technical Guideline 2026 (Approval Date 4 August 2026, Effective Date 1 January 2027) requires that facilities hosting government Level 3 or Level 4 data be "designed, constructed, and operated to a minimum auditable standard" and "deployed according to Uptime Institute Tier III or TIA-942 Rated-3 for both the design and the constructed facility" (§13.1(I)); that power infrastructure "provide a minimum of N+1 redundancy for all critical components, including UPS systems, power distribution units, and backup generators", with PUE reported to NITDA annually (§13.2); and that data hall access require "a minimum of three distinct authentication factors (e.g., key card, PIN code, biometric scan)", with 24x7x365 on-site security personnel mandatory and CCTV retained "for a minimum of 90 days" (§13.3). Two distinct things are being asked for there, and conflating them is the fastest way to lose a technical room: §13.2 is a capacity-component requirement, whereas Uptime Tier III additionally requires concurrent maintainability — redundant distribution paths, such that any component can be taken out of service for maintenance without impacting the IT load. Most of what those clauses describe is a building, a power train, a cooling plant, an earthing system and a physical-security envelope — the disciplines our engineers are registered and trained in, and which Pillars 01 and 02 already describe. We engineer that facility layer only. We are not a cloud service provider, a data centre operator or a systems integrator; we hold no NDIAF Assurance Certification and no Uptime or TIA-942 certification, because those awards attach to a design, a facility and its operations and are made by the licensed issuer, not to a designer or contractor; and we do not host, process or store client data. We have not completed a data centre. Our first full data hall design package would be delivered in joint venture with an established mission-critical design practice, named in the bid.

### Sub-capabilities (cut from ten to four)

**Earthing, bonding, lightning protection and surge survey with remediation design**

Survey of an existing facility's earthing and bonding system, lightning protection and surge protective device coordination, delivered as a defect register with remediation design and priced options. Written to NFPA 780 or IEC 62305, ANSI/TIA-607 for telecommunications bonding and earthing, and UL 1449 for SPDs. This is the lead offer: it is small, it is inside existing MEP competence, it does not require a data centre credential, it is referenceable in weeks, and it sells to commercial buyers (banks, telcos, facility owners) as readily as to federal ones — which matters because the first mission-critical reference will almost certainly not be a defence project.

_Grounded in: Pillar 01 workstream "MEP systems — mechanical, electrical and plumbing design including backup generation, water systems and protected distribution" (tools/content_capabilities.py:239)._

**Standby power, transfer scheme and fuel autonomy engineering**

Load schedules; redundancy topology options presented with their cost and maintainability consequences; generator rating category and transient performance class selection to ISO 8528-1 and ISO 8528-5; switchgear and transfer scheme; fuel storage and day-tank sizing, bunding, polishing and delivery-source reliability; and diesel/gas/solar hybrid options with the NERC captive- or embedded-generation permitting pathway under the Electricity Act 2023 identified so the client's counsel can confirm it. Second lead offer, for the same reasons.

_Grounded in: Pillar 01 MEP workstream (backup generation, protected distribution) and Pillar 02's utility-resilience and backup-generation works._

**Facility compliance gap assessment against the published Nigerian standard**

Walk-through of an existing computer room, server room or data hall and a priced, prioritised delta to the clauses actually published: §13.1 Tier III / Rated-3 topology criteria (stating separately the §13.2 N+1 capacity requirement and Tier III concurrent maintainability), §13.2 PUE reporting, §13.3 three-factor data hall access, 24x7x365 on-site security and 90-day CCTV retention, and NDIAF §11.4 baseline physical infrastructure, physical security and resilience. Delivered with capital cost bands and with upgrade, consolidate and do-not-build presented as live options. Sold with an explicit written position that no regulatory outcome is warranted and that the applicant and its regulatory counsel own any submission to NITDA.

_Grounded in: Pillar 04 "infrastructure risk assessment prioritised by operational consequence and costed for remediation"; Pillar 01 "Design review and assurance — structured multi-discipline reviews with recorded comments and close-out" (tools/content_capabilities.py:242)._

**MEP asset register, criticality ranking and lifecycle cost model**

Plant register for chillers, CRAC/CRAH units, UPS strings and batteries, generators, transfer switches and switchgear; criticality ranking; planned maintenance schedules; a PUE measurement basis the client can report from; and repair/refurbish/replace lifecycle cost models. Handed over in a BIM-for-operations structure so the client's own maintenance organisation inherits usable data and is not dependent on us. Condition monitoring, if instrumented, covers facility plant only and is authorised and monitored separately from any mission network — we monitor plant, not data, and we do not administer systems.

_Grounded in: Pillar 04 "IoT condition monitoring, asset registers and criticality assessment, lifecycle cost analysis, BIM for operations"; Pillar 01 "Asset data schema — the facility data structure the maintenance team will inherit" (tools/content_capabilities.py:262)._

### Must not claim

- Any past performance. Delete "the work we already do", "standards our engineers already work to", and every similar verb. PPEC cannot name one project — about/leadership.html carries "Project references — TODO: assemble reference list". A firm caught inflating past performance on page one is disqualified from everything on page two. The permitted formulation is "the disciplines our engineers are trained and registered in".

- That Tier III means N+1. It does not, and this is the error most likely to be caught. §13.2 requires N+1 on UPS, PDUs and generators — capacity components. Uptime Tier III additionally requires concurrent maintainability, i.e. redundant distribution paths so any component can be shut down for maintenance without impacting IT operation (uptimeinstitute.com/tiers). A facility can be N+1 on a single distribution path and fail Tier III. Stating the distinction is itself the competence signal.

- That PPEC is or could be Tier III certified or TIA-942 accredited. Uptime Institute states it is the only licensed firm that can provide Tier Certifications, and they attach to Design, Construction and Operations. TIA-942 certificates are issued to facilities by TIA-licensed Certification Bodies which must meet ISO/IEC 17020/17021; Design Certification runs one year, Constructed Facility Certification three years with surveillance audits after years 1 and 2. The only honest formulation is "designed to Uptime Tier III / TIA-942 Rated-3 criteria, with the owner's certification submission supported".

- That PPEC is a sovereign cloud provider, cloud service provider, data centre operator, hosting provider or systems integrator; that it holds or has a path to NDIAF Assurance Certification in any class. Verified thresholds (NDIAF Schedule 1 S1.3): Tier 1 Integrated Digital Infrastructure Provider ₦1,000,000,000 paid-up capital and ₦500,000,000 combined PI and cyber liability; Cloud Infrastructure Provider ₦500,000,000 and ₦250,000,000; Cloud Service Provider ₦100,000,000 and ₦250,000,000; CSP (Restricted) ₦25,000,000 and ₦50,000,000; Service Integration Provider ₦10,000,000 and ₦50,000,000 PI. The SIP gate is financially reachable — decline it deliberately, because holding it asserts systems-integration competence PPEC does not have.

- That the Technical Guideline addresses designers or contractors. It does not. Its Application and Scope names FPIs, CSPs, Service Integrators, Data Centre Operators, Managed Service Providers, AI Infrastructure Providers, Sovereign Compute Providers and other Digital Infrastructure Providers — not construction or engineering firms. The proposal's objection about "government contractors and organisations responsible for the design of cloud infrastructure" being named addressees is unsupported and must be dropped. The correct, weaker and still useful citation is the Objectives section, which states the guideline "provides clarity on the technical specifications for project design and public tenders".

- That the NDIAF Operational Manual "will prevail over the Technical Guideline where they conflict". Over-broad and internally inverted. Technical Guideline §13.4 says NDIAF instruments prevail over corresponding procedures, templates or operational requirements in that Guideline. NDIAF Schedule 4 S4.3 says the reverse internally: where the Operational Manual conflicts with the Framework main body or Schedules 1–3, the latter prevail. Also correct the status claim — the Operational Manual is issued as Schedule 4 of the Framework, and S4.2 already lists its mandatory contents including guidance for assessors engaged under §9.8; NITDA is to publish the first edition before the first application window opens.

- "This layer is under-marketed, not already won." AEC and MEP subcontractors are routinely unnamed in data centre announcements; that is normal commercial practice, not evidence of an unserved niche. Either produce a competitor map or state honestly that the competitive position is unknown.

- Any nines figure. No "99.99%", no "52 minutes". Placing an availability number next to your own capability reads as a commitment whether intended or not, and PPEC has no operating record. Replace with: the facility layer sets the ceiling on achievable availability; operating discipline determines whether that ceiling is reached, and that discipline sits with the operator. Do not assert that availability is "an electrical engineering problem before it is an IT problem" — human error during operation and maintenance is a leading outage cause, which is precisely why concurrent maintainability exists.

- Tier III switching-procedure or concurrent-maintainability training for a client's operations team. Highest consequence, lowest competence item in the original proposal. Delete outright. Any training offered now is limited to reading and using the BIM model and asset register PPEC itself produced.

- Colocation cage or fit-out work inside a live certified hall as a starter engagement. It is the hardest first job, gated by operators on approved methods of procedure, change-control history, insurance limits and prior data-hall work. One wrong busway tap-off is a customer outage.

- Independent commissioning provider or owner's engineer appointments. No competent owner appoints a commissioning authority who has never witnessed an integrated systems test on a data hall; the value of the role is recognising a bad result, which is experiential. No commissioning credential was even proposed. These roles also carry the worst liability-to-fee ratio on the list. In the interim offer only commissioning documentation and evidence-file assembly under a named certified commissioning professional.

- Licensed electrical installation with authority-having-jurisdiction inspection records, until the licence and a COREN-registered signing engineer exist and can be quoted by number. Also delete the phrase about "a documented vendor requirement in published site-requirement specifications" — it paraphrases one identifiable vendor's site-requirements document, which an informed reader will name, and the brief forbids vendor branding.

- Galaxy Backbone, or any named prospective client, in anything written or said aloud before there is a contact and a stated requirement. And do not repeat the 825 kW–2,000 kW power / 600 kW–2,800 kW cooling figures; I could not verify them. GBB also has in-house engineering, incumbent vendors and a delivered facility, and its role as aggregation entity creates a conflict if PPEC is simultaneously its subcontractor and bidding through it.

I did not verify: the Uptime ATD fee (~US$4,985), the TIA certified-facility counts (three in Nigeria, eleven in Sub-Saharan Africa), the claim that Nigerian Uptime awards are design-document only, the NERC Q1 2026 statistics (4,458 MW, 32.72% availability factor, 23/27 January collapses, 304.21 kV vs 313.50 kV), the FedRAMP "legacy" date of 24 June 2026, and the claim that Nigeria imports almost all of its mission-critical facility design expertise. The localisation mandate is instrument-specific and level-specific: Level 4 "shall, under no circumstances, be stored or transferred outside Nigeria"; for Level 2 and above both primary and secondary sites must be within Nigeria and in different geopolitical zones; tertiary out-of-country backup is conditionally permissible only for Levels 1 and 2. ### Roadmap fit

Two honest corrections to the roadmap argument before anything useful can be said about fit.

First, the original proposal contradicted itself and the founder will be asked about it. Three paragraphs argued the pillar maps onto the existing three phases "without altering them" and mirrors phase one's low-friction, self-funding logic; the fourth conceded it produces no meaningful revenue for twelve months and must be funded as a five-year positioning play. The published roadmap's own discipline is that each phase earns the next. Only one of those can be said out loud. Say this one: this is a positioning investment funded from outside the phase-by-phase logic, with a capped budget, a named review date and a written kill criterion. Delete the paragraphs claiming it mirrors phase one.

Second, it is not the same go-to-market. The published roadmap sells to unit commanders, the Army and MoD through defence procurement. The facility-compliance buyers are MDA CIOs and facility heads, NITDA, and — realistically first — commercial owners: a Lagos or Abuja data centre operator's expansion phase, a bank, a telco. Different customer, different budget line, different procurement route, different competitor set. Calling it "the same three phases" conceals a second go-to-market opened on the same founder's calendar while Pillars 1–4 have still delivered nothing. State the opportunity cost explicitly in the internal plan.

With that said, the fit is real but narrower than proposed.

PHASE ONE. The four sub-capabilities above sit alongside hardware-selection consultation and supply chain stabilisation as a parallel non-sensitive, no-clearance, professional-fees workstream: earthing and surge surveys, standby power and fuel autonomy studies, compliance gap assessments, asset registers and lifecycle cost models. They require no technical accreditation — but they do require the four commercial registrations, which is the real gate on the timeline and which the original proposal treated as a wish-list bullet.

PHASE TWO. Full FEED-to-IFC data hall design and paired-site feasibility across two geopolitical zones belong here, not now, and the first design package goes out in joint venture with a named practice. Contract forms move in step: consultant appointments and FIDIC Short Form scale first; employer-designed construction later; EPC/turnkey not at all until there is a completed reference and bonding capacity. Signing a fixed-price turnkey contract for a first Tier III facility — a form deliberately run without an Engineer — remains the single most likely way this service line damages the firm.

PHASE THREE. Indigenous accredited mission-critical facility engineering, local assembly and integration of modular computing enclosures with the maintenance hubs that sustain them, university partnerships for data centre MEP, controls and commissioning skills, and critical facilities management on a facility PPEC itself built. This is where the ambition honestly lives.

On timing: do not sell against 1 January 2027. It is four months away and PPEC cannot be credentialled inside it — using urgency from a standing start hands the buyer the objection "then you are too late." Position instead on the multi-year remediation tail: a compliance deadline landing on an under-funded federal estate produces years of assessment and upgrade work after the date, which suits a firm that will be ready in 2027 rather than 2026.

On the reference path: accept in writing, internally, that the first mission-critical reference will probably be commercial rather than defence, priced to win rather than to profit. If commercial references are unacceptable to the founder, there is no reference path and phases two and three never open — that is a decision, not a detail.

### Objections and answers

**You have never built a data centre. Why would we let you near ours?**

We would not bid as principal contractor on a first Tier III facility, and we say so before you ask. What we sell now is narrower than that and is measured against published criteria rather than a portfolio: earthing, bonding, lightning and surge survey with remediation design; standby power, transfer scheme and fuel autonomy engineering; a priced compliance gap assessment against §13.1 to §13.3; and an asset register and lifecycle cost model. On a full design package we would joint-venture with an established mission-critical design practice and name them in the bid. We have completed no data centre, we have no Accredited Tier Designer on staff yet, and both facts are written on our own page.

**Are you Tier III certified? Is your firm TIA-942 accredited?**

No, and no engineering or construction firm can be. Uptime Institute states it is the only licensed firm that can issue Tier Certifications, and they attach to design documents, the constructed facility and its operations. TIA-942 certificates are issued to facilities by TIA-licensed Certification Bodies, which must meet ISO/IEC 17020 or 17021; the Design Certification runs one year and the Constructed Facility Certification three, with surveillance audits after years one and two. What we can honestly say is that we design to Tier III and Rated-3 criteria and support the owner's certification submission. Anyone telling you their construction firm 'is Tier III' is describing something that does not exist.

**Your page cites N+1 as if that were Tier III. Which is it?**

They are two different requirements and we state them separately for that reason. §13.2 requires a minimum of N+1 redundancy for critical components including UPS systems, power distribution units and backup generators — that is capacity. Uptime Tier III adds concurrent maintainability: redundant distribution paths, so any component can be taken out of service for planned maintenance without impacting the IT load. A facility can be fully N+1 on a single distribution path and still fail Tier III. If a bidder's documents treat the two as the same thing, that is a useful signal about the rest of their submission.

**This is a cloud capability. You are an engineering firm out of your lane.**

Sovereignty decomposes into separable layers — legal entity and jurisdiction, personnel screening, logical and cryptographic controls, and the physical facility. We sell only the fourth, and we do not sell all of it. Nigeria's own technical standard is written largely in facility language: topology certification for design and constructed facility, N+1 on UPS, PDUs and generators, three distinct authentication factors at data hall entry, 24x7x365 on-site security personnel, 90-day CCTV retention. ANSI/TIA-942-C's declared scope covers site location, building structure, electrical and mechanical infrastructure, fire safety and physical security. That is the lane. We do not touch the platform, we do not hold keys, and we do not host, process or store data.

**Why you and not a systems integrator, who could deliver the whole thing?**

They are separately procured roles. The NDIAF certifies providers who own, operate or hold enforceable rights over facilities, and service integrators working under a certified provider; it creates no certification class for designers or builders at all. Note also that NDIAF §11.3(c) states expressly that a Design Assurance Review 'does not replace any environmental, telecommunications, energy, or construction approval required under other applicable laws' — the regulator itself puts construction and engineering competence alongside and outside the cloud certification. On a bid we expect to sit under a certified provider or integrator as the facilities and engineering party, not to displace them. We could reach the Service Integration Provider financial threshold — ₦10,000,000 capital and ₦50,000,000 professional indemnity — and we decline it deliberately, because holding it would assert systems-integration competence we do not have.

**Do you have cleared staff? Are you approved by ONSA?**

No to both. Level 4 data must reside exclusively on-premises within the institution, in a collocated private data centre, or in a NITDA-certified private cloud inside Nigeria, and the hosting infrastructure must comply with security standards defined and audited by the Office of the National Security Adviser. Those standards are not public, we do not hold them, and we do not interpret or substitute for them. On a Level 4 facility our role would be subcontract to a cleared prime. What we do operate is a documented baseline screening standard of our own — identity, right to work, employment history, unspent convictions — with escort discipline, least-privilege time-bound site access with automatic expiry, and documented maintenance records. Clearances are sponsored by the customer's security authority and cannot be self-applied.

**Is there actually a market, or is this a policy fashion?**

I can show you the mandate and I will not overstate the demand. The National Guideline for Cloud Computing in Nigeria 2026 and the National Cloud Technical Guideline 2026 were both signed on 4 August 2026 with an effective date of 1 January 2027, and together they require Tier III or Rated-3 for design and constructed facility for all government Level 3 and Level 4 data, with primary and secondary sites in different geopolitical zones for Level 2 and above. What I cannot show you is a funded programme: no Nigerian defence-specific data centre or cloud programme is publicly confirmed, and I have not seen one. I also will not sell you the deadline as urgency — we will not be fully credentialled before it, and the work that matters is the multi-year remediation tail on the existing estate afterwards. If the mandate is not enough for you, the right first engagement is a survey, not a construction contract.

**You are promising high availability on the Nigerian grid. That is not credible.**

We are not promising it and we publish no availability figure. The facility layer sets a ceiling on achievable availability; whether that ceiling is reached is determined by operating discipline, and that sits with the operator, not with us — human error during maintenance is a leading cause of critical-facility outages, which is precisely why concurrent maintainability exists as a concept. What we do is engineer to a documented basis: load schedule, redundancy topology with its cost and maintainability consequences stated, generator rating category and transient performance class to ISO 8528-1 and ISO 8528-5, transfer scheme, fuel storage and day-tank sizing, and the captive- or embedded-generation permitting pathway identified for your counsel to confirm. Grid conditions are why that is the lead workstream rather than a footnote, and I will give you current figures in the briefing rather than on a web page, because they go stale within weeks.

**Data residency does not solve jurisdiction. A foreign-owned provider can still be compelled to disclose data wherever it sits.**

Correct, and it is an argument for the facility rather than against it. NITDA's own position is that infrastructure is not deemed sovereign on the basis of physical location, ownership structure or commercial description alone. We therefore sell no sovereignty guarantee and no jurisdictional assurance. We deliver a facility that any NITDA-certified provider can occupy, which is the asset that survives a change of platform, a re-competition or a policy reversal. That is a stronger position for an engineering firm than for an integrator, and it is the only claim we make.

**Cloud-first policy exists to get government out of running its own data centres. You are selling the duplication the policy is meant to remove.**

Fair, and it is why the first engagement is an assessment rather than a design. Our first questions are whether a new facility is needed at all, whether an existing one can be upgraded or consolidated into, and what total cost of ownership looks like including embedded generation and fuel. We are prepared to recommend not building, and 'do not build' is a written outcome option in the assessment scope. Where the data is Level 4 the guideline forecloses the commercial cloud option anyway, so the choice there is between a facility built properly and one built badly.

**A hyperscaler or an international contractor will take this work. What do you bring that they do not?**

They may well take the platform, and possibly the building too on a first-of-type facility. What I can point to is a regulatory incentive rather than a claim about ourselves: Annex H of the Technical Guideline, which rates a foreign provider's Nigerian data centre investment, sets its highest category as direct capital investment in a hyperscale region, facilities certified Uptime Tier III or TIA-942 Rated-3 for design and constructed facility, and 'demonstrable use of local construction, engineering, and facilities management firms'. That points at firms like ours. We are indigenous, we are in Abuja, and we can reach a second geopolitical zone once we have a named partner there — which we do not yet. Our realistic ambition is to be the named local engineering firm in someone else's investment dossier, not to displace them.

**Who runs the facility after handover? And are you not proposing to be designer, builder, assurer and maintainer of the same asset?**

The client or a certified operator runs it. Our operations offer covers facility plant only — chillers, air handling, UPS strings and batteries, generators, fuel levels, transfer switches, leak detection, door states — instrumented and authorised separately from any mission network. We monitor plant, not data; we do not administer systems. And on the role-stacking point, which is the sharper question: we publish a rule that on any one facility we hold either the design and build role or an assurance role, never both, and that we do not qualify vendors for plant we will ourselves install and maintain without your written waiver. We hand over an asset register, a BIM model structured for operations and a lifecycle cost model so your engineers are not dependent on us.

**Isn't 'sovereign cloud' just marketing, and isn't this an engineering firm chasing a policy window?**

The term is contested, which is why we do not sell it. We sell a building measured against criteria you can check yourself in an afternoon: §13.1 to §13.3 of a published Nigerian guideline, NDIAF §11.3 and §11.4, ANSI/TIA-942-C, Uptime's Tier definitions, ISO 8528, NFPA 780. On the second point, this is a named service line inside our existing engineering design pillar rather than a new pillar or a rebrand, deliberately — we would rather publish four capabilities we can staff than five we cannot. And we hold a verification file with the clause and retrieval date behind every figure we quote. Ask for it.
